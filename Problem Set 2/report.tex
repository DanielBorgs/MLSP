\section*{Problem 2.1}
The standard filtering operation using a Finite Impulse Response (FIR) filter is defined by the discrete convolution sum. The output $y_n$ at time step $n$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
    y_n = \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \theta_l x_{n-l}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_l$ are the filter coefficients for $l = 0, 1, \dots, L-1$. 

To express this operation as a matrix-vector product $y = X\theta$, we structure the filter coefficients as a column vector and the input signal as a matrix where each row represents the time-delayed samples of the input signal interacting with the filter.

The filter coefficients are arranged into an $L \times 1$ column vector $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
    \theta = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \theta_0 \\
        \theta_1 \\
        \vdots \\
        \theta_{L-1}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

To satisfy the convolution sum, each row of the matrix $X$ must contain the current and past values of the signal $x_n$, shifted by one time step per column. Assuming we compute the valid convolution where we only compute outputs for time indices where the filter fully overlaps with the available data (i.e., $n \in [L-1, N-1]$), $X$ is constructed as an $(N - L + 1) \times L$ matrix:
\begin{equation}
    X = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{L-1} & x_{L-2} & \dots  & x_0 \\
        x_{L}   & x_{L-1} & \dots  & x_1 \\
        x_{L+1} & x_{L}   & \dots  & x_2 \\
        \vdots  & \vdots  & \ddots & \vdots \\
        x_{N-1} & x_{N-2} & \dots  & x_{N-L}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

If we assume a full convolution where zero-padding is used for $x_n$ outside the range $0 \le n < N$, the matrix $X$ would be expanded with zeros at the boundaries, resulting in an $(N + L - 1) \times L$ matrix:
\begin{equation}
    X_{full} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_0    & 0       & \dots  & 0 \\
        x_1    & x_0     & \dots  & 0 \\
        \vdots & \vdots  & \ddots & \vdots \\
        x_{N-1}& x_{N-2} & \dots  & x_{N-L} \\
        0      & x_{N-1} & \dots  & x_{N-L+1} \\
        \vdots & \vdots  & \ddots & \vdots \\
        0      & 0       & \dots  & x_{N-1}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

With $X$ and $\theta$ defined, the filtering operation is executed via matrix multiplication, yielding the output column vector $y$:
\begin{equation}
    y = X\theta = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        y_{L-1} \\
        y_{L} \\
        \vdots \\
        y_{N-1}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
Each element of $y$ corresponds exactly to the dot product of a row in $X$ and the vector $\theta$, thus flawlessly replicating the convolution sum.

\section*{Problem 2.2}
We begin with the definition of the Mean Squared Error (MSE) and add/subtract the expected value of the estimator, $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]$, inside the square:
\begin{align}
    \text{MSE} &= \mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta} - \theta_o)^2\right] \nonumber \\
    &= \mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] + \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta_o)^2\right]
\end{align}
Expanding the square yields three terms:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MSE} = \mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}])^2\right] + \mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta_o)^2\right] + 2\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}])(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta_o)\right]
\end{equation}

The first term is the standard definition of the estimator's variance: $\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}])^2\right]$. 
The second term consists of non-random constants, so $\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta_o)^2\right] = (\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta_o)^2 = \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2$. 
For the cross-term, the term $(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta_o)$ is a constant with respect to the outer expectation and can be pulled out: $2(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta_o) \cdot \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]]$. Because $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]] = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] = 0$, the entire cross-term evaluates to zero.

Summing these parts gives the standard decomposition:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MSE} = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2
\end{equation}

An unbiased estimator inherently satisfies $\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = 0$, reducing its MSE entirely to the estimator's variance: $\text{MSE}_{unbiased} = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta})$. 

A biased estimator is often better suited for minimizing the overall MSE. Introducing a small, controlled amount of bias can act as a regularization mechanism. This restricts the model's sensitivity to noisy data, which can reduce its variance. If the reduction in variance exceeds the penalty of the squared bias introduced, the overall MSE will decrease:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}_{biased}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}_{biased}(\hat{\theta})^2 < \text{Var}_{unbiased}(\hat{\theta})
\end{equation}
Because the MSE is a sum of strictly non-negative terms, allowing a non-zero bias lets us exploit this bias-variance tradeoff to achieve a lower total error than would be mathematically possible if we strictly enforced $\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = 0$.

\section*{Problem 2.3}
A function $f$ defined on a convex set $\mathcal{C}$ is convex if, for all $\theta_1,\theta_2\in\mathcal{C}$ and all $\lambda\in[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}
    f\big(\lambda\theta_1+(1-\lambda)\theta_2\big)\le \lambda f(\theta_1)+(1-\lambda)f(\theta_2).
\end{equation}
The domain $\mathbb{R}^l$ of a norm is a convex set. Let $\|\cdot\|_p$ satisfy the three norm properties and take $\theta_1,\theta_2\in\mathbb{R}^l$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$. Then
\begin{align}
    \|\lambda\theta_1+(1-\lambda)\theta_2\|_p
    &\le \|\lambda\theta_1\|_p+\|(1-\lambda)\theta_2\|_p \nonumber\\
    &= |\lambda|\,\|\theta_1\|_p+|1-\lambda|\,\|\theta_2\|_p \nonumber\\
    &= \lambda\|\theta_1\|_p+(1-\lambda)\|\theta_2\|_p \nonumber
\end{align}
This is exactly the definition of convexity, so every norm is convex.

\section*{Problem 2.4}
The autocorrelation at time instants $n,m$ is defined as $r(n,m)=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{u}_n\mathrm{u}_m]$ (eq.\ 2.101). For a wide-sense stationary process it only depends on the lag $k=n-m$, so we write $r(k)=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{u}_n\mathrm{u}_{n-k}]$.

Since $|a|<1$ the process is wide-sense stationary. The noise $\mathrm{v}_n$ is white and $\mathrm{x}_{n-k}$ depends only on $\mathrm{v}_m$ with $m\le n-k$. Hence $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{v}_n\mathrm{x}_{n-k}]=0$ for $k\ge1$, and $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{v}_n\mathrm{x}_n]=\sigma_v^2$.

For $k=0$:
\begin{equation}
    r_x(0)=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{x}_n(a\mathrm{x}_{n-1}+\mathrm{v}_n)]=a\,r_x(1)+\sigma_v^2 .
\end{equation}
For $k\ge1$:
\begin{equation}
    r_x(k)=\mathbb{E}[(a\mathrm{x}_{n-1}+\mathrm{v}_n)\mathrm{x}_{n-k}]=a\,r_x(k-1)\quad\Rightarrow\quad r_x(k)=a^{k}r_x(0).
\end{equation}
Inserting $r_x(1)=a\,r_x(0)$ in the first equation gives $r_x(0)=a^2r_x(0)+\sigma_v^2$, which means:
\begin{equation}
    r_x(0)=\frac{\sigma_v^2}{1-a^2}.
\end{equation}
Because $r_x(-k)=r_x(k)$, we obtain for all $k$:
\begin{equation}
    r_x(k)=\frac{\sigma_v^2}{1-a^2}\,a^{|k|}.
\end{equation}

Using $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{x}_n]=0$, we find the autocorrelation of $\mathrm{y}_n$ with $m=n-k$:
\begin{align}
    r_y(n,n-k)&=\mathbb{E}[(\mathrm{x}_n+b)(\mathrm{x}_{n-k}+b)]
    =r_x(k)+b\,\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{x}_n]+b\,\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{x}_{n-k}]+b^2\nonumber\\
    &=\frac{\sigma_v^2}{1-a^2}\,a^{|k|}+b^2 .
\end{align}
The result only depends on the lag $k$ and $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{y}_n]=b$ is constant, so $\mathrm{y}_n$ is wide-sense stationary and $r_y(n,n-k)=r_y(k)$. The offset adds the constant $b^2$ at every lag. For $a=0.8$, $b=0.5$, and $\sigma_v^2=1$:
\begin{equation}
    r_y(-2)=r_y(n,n+2)=\frac{1}{1-0.64}\,0.8^{2}+0.25=\frac{0.64}{0.36}+0.25=\frac{16}{9}+\frac14=\frac{73}{36}\approx 2.03 .
\end{equation}

\section*{Problem 2.5}
In a filter setup, the filter acts on the input $x_n$ and produces the output $\hat d_n=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\theta_l x_{n-l}=\theta^\top \boldsymbol{x}_n$. The desired signal $d_n$ is the signal we would like the output to reproduce, and the error signal is the difference:
\begin{equation}
    e_n=d_n-\hat d_n=d_n-\theta^\top\boldsymbol{x}_n .
\end{equation}
The filter is chosen to minimize $\mathbb{E}[e_n^2]$. In this problem, the input is $x_n=s_n+w_n$, the desired signal is the clean signal $d_n=s_n$, and the error is $e_n=s_n-\hat s_n$, which is the remaining noise plus the distortion of the signal. 

Setting the gradient of $J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[(d_n-\theta^\top\boldsymbol{x}_n)^2]$ to zero gives the normal equations:
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_x\,\theta_*=p,\qquad [\Sigma_x]_{ij}=r_x(i-j),\qquad p_k=\mathbb{E}[d_n x_{n-k}]=r_{dx}(k).
\end{equation}
Since $s_n$ and $w_n$ are uncorrelated, $\Sigma_x=\Sigma_s+\Sigma_w$ and $p_k=r_s(k)$. In the frequency domain, for $L\to\infty$, the solution is:
\begin{equation}
    H(f)=\frac{S_s(f)}{S_s(f)+S_w(f)},
\end{equation}
which passes frequencies where the signal dominates and attenuates those where the noise dominates. 

The filter must be long enough to resolve the spectral structure of $S_s/(S_s+S_w)$ since a longer filter has a finer frequency resolution and approximates the ideal response better, meaning the MSE decreases with $L$. However, $\Sigma_x$ and $p$ are estimated from a finite number of samples, and the number of parameters grows with $L$. Estimating the filter on the first half of the data and measuring the MSE on the second half gives a chosen length of $L=\resWienerL$ (Figure \ref{fig:p25mse}).

Using all samples to estimate the filter, the signal-to-noise ratio is improved from \resWienerSNRin\,dB at the input to \resWienerSNRout\,dB at the output, a gain of \resWienerGain\,dB. Figure \ref{fig:p25freq} shows the frequency response of the Wiener filter together with the ideal response. The filter approximates the ideal response with a gain close to 0 dB where the signal spectrum lies above the noise spectrum, and strong attenuation where the noise dominates.

\section*{Problem 2.6}
The signal is sampled at $f_s=\resSixFs$ Hz and lasts \resSixDur\,s. The short-time Fourier transform is subject to a time and frequency resolution trade-off where a window of $N_w$ samples gives a frequency resolution of about $f_s/N_w$ and a time resolution of about $N_w/f_s$. A window length of 1024 samples gives a resolution of about \resSixDf\,Hz, which is fine enough to separate the low-frequency drum from drums with energy at higher frequencies. The window is short compared to the time between the beats so successive beats do not smear into each other. A Hann window is used because it has low side lobes so the strong low-frequency components do not leak into the weak high-frequency content. An overlap of 87.5\% is used to avoid losing information at the window edges and to give a smooth time axis and precise localization of the beats.

The beats were found as peaks of the spectral flux (Figure \ref{fig:p26spec}), detecting \resSixNons{} beats with a median time between consecutive beats of \resSixIOI\,s, corresponding to a tempo of about \resSixBPM{} beats per minute. The 87.5\% overlap gives a time resolution of \resSixHopMs\,ms in the localization of the beats.

\section*{Problem 2.7}
The signals in the setup are the source signal which is the speech $s_n$ that we want to recover, the unknown linear system $h$ that the noise passes through, the primary microphone signal $p_n=s_n+(h*v)_n$ which is the desired signal, the reference microphone signal $v_n$ which is the input $x_n$, the adaptive FIR filter $w$, the filter output $a_n$ which is an estimate of the noise component, the error signal $e_n=p_n-a_n$ which is the output of the whole system, and the update of the filter coefficients $c$.

The adaptive algorithm minimizes the power of the error $e_n=p_n-a_n$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[e_n^2]=\mathbb{E}\big[(s_n+n_n-a_n)^2\big]
    =\mathbb{E}[s_n^2]+\mathbb{E}\big[(n_n-a_n)^2\big]+2\,\mathbb{E}\big[s_n(n_n-a_n)\big].
\end{equation}
If the speech is uncorrelated with the noise, the cross-term vanishes and $\mathbb{E}[e_n^2]=\mathbb{E}[s_n^2]+\mathbb{E}\big[(n_n-a_n)^2\big]$. Minimizing the output power is the same as minimizing the noise residual $\mathbb{E}[(n_n-a_n)^2]$, which is minimized for $w=h$, and then $e_n=s_n$. If the speech is correlated with the reference, the filter reduces the output power by also cancelling parts of the speech itself and the output is then a distorted speech signal.

The speech signal was scaled to a peak value of 0.5, and the reference noise $v_n$ is white Gaussian noise filtered through low-pass, high-pass, and band-pass impulse responses. The adaptive filters are LMS, NLMS, and RLS. RLS converges in the order of $M$ to $2M$ samples independently of the eigenvalue spread but at the cost of $O(M^2)$ operations per sample. LMS and NLMS cost $O(M)$ and converge more slowly, while NLMS removes the dependence on the input power through normalization so it is easier to tune (Figure \ref{fig:p27learn}).
